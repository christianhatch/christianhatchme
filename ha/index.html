The design goal

The whole point is that you never see the machinery.

No sensor pods staring down from every corner like a bank lobby. No tablet to unlock, no app to find, no deciding which of four remotes it was. The house notices you're there, does the obvious thing, and gets out of the way.

And when you do want something different — dimmer, warmer, quieter, off — there's a switch exactly where your hand already went looking for one. That's the whole interface. Everything else is optional.

Sensors hidden in trim, fixtures and furniture Nothing new to unlock, learn or charge No screen on a wall unless you want one

What it actually buys you

Not gadgets. Fewer things to keep track of.

Most "smart homes" hand you thirty apps and a new chore. Done properly it works the other way round: a shorter list of things you have to hold in your head, and a house that raises its hand when something is genuinely wrong.

Leaving & arriving

One button empties the house

Lights, music, space heaters, the AC in the room you forgot about, the lamp in the closet. One tap on the way out the door — or automatically, when the last person leaves. From the airport you can see the front door is locked, not guess.

Running at the reference house: Leave Home · Everything Off · Good Night, with a 2 a.m. safety net that runs it anyway if nobody did.

Light that follows you

Rooms lit before you walk in

Sensors that know you're there, not just that you moved — so the light stays on while you sit reading in a chair, and goes off on a delay you choose. Closets and staircases light on the door. At night everything comes up dim and warm instead of blinding you.

Running at the reference house: lights that follow you through 12 rooms, each on its own timing, and one setting that stands the whole thing down for guests.

Cost, made visible

You know what each thing costs

Not one number for the whole house — a number per appliance, in dollars, updated live. The chest freezer. The EV charger. The dehumidifier that runs all August. It turns arguments about the electric bill into a two-second look.

Running at the reference house: 33 appliances individually metered for power, energy and running cost, plus whole-house water.

Air and comfort

The room fixes itself

The bathroom fan runs on how damp the room actually is, not a timer. Stale air in the office nudges you before the headache does. Purifiers and humidifiers hold the level you asked for, not just a fan speed. Sun-facing shades close ahead of the afternoon heat.

Running at the reference house: air-quality alerts, a bathroom fan that reacts to steam, humidifiers that hold a set level, and shades that beat the sun in three rooms.

Things that go wrong quietly

You hear about it first

The freezer that got unplugged behind the shelf. The washing machine that finished two hours ago. A pipe leaking in a basement nobody visits. The moisture absorber in a storage bin that quietly gave up three weeks ago. These are the failures that cost real money precisely because nothing announces them.

Running at the reference house: a freezer that reports its own temperature, an alert if anything critical loses power, leak detection under every water line, laundry-finished alerts, and a three-day damp check on long-term storage.

Music & atmosphere

Music that follows the room

Speakers group and ungroup on their own. Sound pauses in a room once it's empty and picks up where you left it. A single control at the door does light, volume and shades — no phone, no voice assistant, nothing to explain to a guest.

Running at the reference house: music in 10 rooms, pausing itself in 8 of them, and real dimmers and remotes in every one.

Non-negotiables

Eight rules I don't bend.

These aren't preferences. They're the constraints every design gets held to, and they're the reason a house I build still feels like a house — not a product you have to operate.

Nothing requires an app.

The phone is a convenience, never the thing your house depends on. If a feature only exists inside an app, it doesn't exist. You should be able to run your entire house having never downloaded anything.

Every device keeps a physical control.

A switch, a dimmer, or a remote, within reach of where you'd actually use it. If the network went down tonight, the lights are the last place you'd find out.

A guest should never know.

Dimmers on the wall where a dimmer belongs. Nothing switching on or off unless a person asked for it. Someone visiting for a weekend should use your house exactly as they'd use any other, and never be told a single thing about it.

Everything runs locally.

Every automation, every schedule, every sensor runs on a computer in your house. The cloud is optional, and you can cut it off entirely. No manufacturer's outage becomes your problem, and no company going under takes your lighting with it.

Full control from anywhere.

Everything you can do standing in the kitchen, you can do from a hotel room in another timezone — over a private, encrypted connection to your own house, not through some company's servers.

Your whole network travels with you.

A private VPN back to the house means being abroad is the same as sitting at your own desk. Your files, your cameras, your machines — all reachable, and reachable only by you.

Privacy is a design constraint, not a setting.

What happens in your house stays in your house. Nothing gets sold, studied, or handed to a company as the hidden price of a feature. Security is built into the network itself, not bolted on afterwards.

All the data. None of the noise.

Every device's full history is there the moment you want it — years of it, down to the watt. But the everyday view shows six things and nothing else. Depth on demand, never depth by default.

The first three rules are the hard ones.

How I work

Opinionated, and boring on purpose.

A smart home fails the same way every time: it gets built out of clever workarounds, then the person who built it leaves. I build the plain version — the one that still makes sense to somebody else in five years.

No hacks, no house of cards

If a feature needs a fragile workaround to exist, I'll tell you what it would cost to keep alive and let you decide. Usually the honest answer is that the feature isn't worth it.

You approve before I touch anything

Every change is planned, explained, and confirmed with you first — then verified afterwards. Nothing gets quietly rewired while you're at work.

One source of truth

No parallel systems that half-agree. Every setting is written down, dated and reversible, and all of it is yours — including if you ever fire me.

Tuned to your actual life

The pets that set off the sensors. The tenant whose shower throws off the water readings. The room that's ten degrees hotter every August. Standard installs ignore these. They're the entire job.
