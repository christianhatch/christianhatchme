House History & Architectural Analysis

29–31 Fenwick Street, Somerville, Massachusetts

Executive Summary

The house at 29–31 Fenwick Street, Somerville, Massachusetts was built in 1903, with first occupancy in late 1903 / early 1904.

This date is established through documentary evidence — specifically, listings in the Somerville City Directories — and is corroborated by historical maps, architectural and material analysis, and the documented neighborhood development pattern of Winter Hill in the early 20th century.

The building is an early-twentieth-century purpose-built urban duplex constructed during Somerville's post-1900 residential infill wave, immediately following the extension of Fenwick Street through to Jacques Street.

The structure retains numerous original features and systems consistent with this construction date:

  • Original stained glass windows
  • Transitional late-Victorian / Edwardian trim
  • Stone foundation
  • Steam heating infrastructure
  • Lead service piping remnants
  • Remnants of an earlier gravity warm-air duct system
  • A Philadelphia-style vertically split duplex layout

Primary Dating Evidence: Somerville City Directories

The Somerville City Directories provide definitive evidence of the construction date.

Method

The Somerville City Directories were published annually beginning in 1851. Editions from 1869 to 1919 are digitized and freely accessible via the Somerville Public Library's local history collection. Each directory lists residents alphabetically by surname and includes a separate “Streets” section listing households by address.

By searching consecutive directory editions for any listing at “29 Fenwick Street” or “31 Fenwick Street,” the year of first occupancy can be determined directly.

Interpretation

City directories of this era were compiled in the late summer and fall of the year preceding publication, capturing residents present during the canvass.

The first appearance of 29 / 31 Fenwick in the 1904 directory therefore indicates:

  • Construction completed in 1903
  • First occupancy in late 1903 / early 1904
  • Captured in directory canvass of late 1903
  • Published in early 1904

This is the standard pattern for new construction documented in city directories of this period.

Significance

The directory listings pinpoint construction to 1903, providing the documentary anchor against which all other evidence — maps, architectural features, and material remnants — can be evaluated.

Architectural Context

Leading Edge of an Aesthetic Transition

The house exhibits stained glass and trim consistent with a transitional late-Victorian / Edwardian / early Arts-and-Crafts aesthetic typically dated to approximately 1905–1912. Constructed in 1903, the house sits at the leading edge of this aesthetic shift rather than the middle of it.

This is not unusual for higher-quality speculative construction in growing urban markets. Builders catering to upwardly mobile middle-class tenants frequently adopted emerging aesthetic trends a year or two ahead of broader market saturation.

Neighborhood Context

The house was constructed during the documented post-1900 residential infill wave in Winter Hill. According to the 1980 Massachusetts Historical Commission Reconnaissance Survey for Somerville, the city's late-19th and early-20th-century suburban growth produced “Colonial Revival and Queen Anne two-family houses” as the predominant housing type, with construction continuing along trolley-served corridors throughout the Late Industrial Period (1870–1915).

The 1903 construction date places 29–31 Fenwick Street near the early end of the post-extension infill wave on Fenwick Street itself, immediately after the street was extended through to Jacques Street.

Architectural Description

General Building Description

29–31 Fenwick Street is a purpose-built early-twentieth-century two-family wood-frame dwelling situated on a narrow urban lot in the Winter Hill neighborhood of Somerville, Massachusetts.

The building is approximately 2½ stories in height and rests on a stone foundation. The structure features:

  • Front-facing gable roof
  • Asymmetrical façade
  • Full-width front porch
  • Bay projections
  • Original wood clapboard and shingle cladding beneath later alterations

Architectural Context

The building reflects the transition from late Victorian / Queen Anne influences toward simpler Edwardian-era residential architecture. This is consistent with the architectural patterns documented for Somerville's Late Industrial Period (1870–1915).

Duplex Layout Analysis

Building Configuration

The house follows a Philadelphia-style vertically split duplex layout rather than a stacked-flat arrangement.

Layout Characteristics

Unit 1 (29 Fenwick)

  • Occupies the first floor
  • Includes an additional bedroom on the second floor
  • Has its own private entrance

Unit 2 (31 Fenwick)

  • Occupies the remainder of the second floor
  • Occupies the entirety of the third floor
  • Has its own private entrance

Significance

This layout style is strongly associated with:

  • Early-twentieth-century speculative duplex construction
  • Owner-occupied rental housing
  • Dense urban infill development
  • c. 1900–1915 urban housing patterns

Interior Architectural Features

Trim & Woodwork

The surviving trim and molding profiles are transitional in character and consistent with early-twentieth-century Edwardian-era simplification.

Characteristics

  • Wide flat casings
  • Stepped back-banding
  • Restrained ornamentation
  • Decorative applied brackets over openings
  • Simplified late-Victorian detailing

Architectural Interpretation

The trim style reflects:

  • A move away from heavily ornate Victorian interiors
  • A pre-Craftsman transitional aesthetic
  • Typical middle-class speculative housing finishes of the early 1900s

Original Stained Glass

Description

The house retains original decorative stained-glass windows featuring:

  • Muted lavender / purple tones
  • Aqua and pale green tones
  • Geometric and organic leaded patterns
  • Symmetrical compositions
  • Non-figurative decorative motifs

Architectural Interpretation

The stained glass is consistent with:

  • Edwardian residential design
  • Arts-and-Crafts influence
  • Early-twentieth-century middle-class urban housing

The windows are stylistically inconsistent with:

  • High Victorian stained glass
  • Gothic Revival designs
  • Art Deco work
  • Later geometric Craftsman leaded glass

Significance

The stained glass at 29–31 Fenwick Street represents an early example of this transitional aesthetic. Installation in 1903 places it at the leading edge of when this style of leaded decorative glass was being adopted in Boston-area residential construction. This suggests the original builder targeted a higher-quality finish to appeal to upwardly mobile middle-class tenants or owners.

Heating & Mechanical System History

Original Steam Heating System

The building currently uses a steam radiator heating system.

Surviving Evidence

  • Steam radiators
  • Boiler piping infrastructure

Historical Interpretation

Steam systems of this type became common in urban residential construction between approximately 1890 and 1920. Steam was an established and increasingly common heating technology by 1903.

Remnants of Early Gravity Warm-Air Heating System

The basement contains remnants of an earlier gravity warm-air duct system.

Physical Evidence

  • Large sheet-metal duct trunks
  • Hand-formed duct transitions
  • Abandoned vertical duct runs within wall cavities
  • Crushed and capped sheet-metal ducts

Interpretation

These ducts likely belonged to:

  • A gravity warm-air furnace system (“octopus furnace” style)
  • A very early forced-air heating system, predating or overlapping with steam conversion

Significance

The surviving ducts:

  • Are consistent with an original 1903 construction date
  • Reflect transitional heating technologies of the era
  • Suggest the house underwent at least one major heating-system modernization

Electrical & Plumbing Evidence

Knob-and-Tube Wiring

The home originally had knob-and-tube wiring but no longer does — it has since been removed and replaced with modern wiring.

Significance

Knob-and-tube wiring was commonly installed between approximately 1890–1930. Its original presence in the house is consistent with a 1903 electrical installation.

Lead Water Service Remnants

The house contains evidence of earlier lead piping. Lead service lines were commonly used in urban housing prior to approximately 1920, fully consistent with the 1903 construction date.

Foundation & Structural Evidence

Stone Foundation

The building rests on a stone foundation.

Historical Interpretation

Stone foundations remained common in New England residential construction through the early 1900s before poured concrete foundations became dominant. The 1903 construction date is at the late end of stone foundation use; many comparable buildings constructed even a few years later used poured concrete foundations.

Historical Interpretation & Urban Context

29–31 Fenwick Street was constructed during the post-1900 residential infill wave in Winter Hill, Somerville. Its construction in 1903 immediately followed the extension of Fenwick Street through to Jacques Street.

The building represents an early example of the house type — Colonial Revival / late Queen Anne speculative duplex — that came to dominate Somerville's residential fabric during the Late Industrial Period (1870–1915), as documented by the Massachusetts Historical Commission.

The building likely functioned as:

  • A speculative duplex investment property
  • An owner-occupied income-producing residence
  • A middle-class urban housing type associated with early transit-oriented suburban growth (Winter Hill was served by the Broadway streetcar line)

The house reflects:

  • The decline of high-Victorian ornamentation
  • The rise of more restrained Edwardian aesthetics
  • Increasing urban density in Somerville
  • The growing importance of rental income as part of middle-class homeownership

Final Conclusion

29–31 Fenwick Street, Somerville, Massachusetts was built in 1903, with first occupancy in late 1903 or early 1904.

This date is established by listings in the Somerville City Directory and corroborated by:

  • 1895 Bromley Atlas (lot vacant)
  • 1900 Sanborn Fire Insurance Map (lot vacant; Fenwick not yet extended)
  • 1903 Somerville City Directory (no listing at 29 or 31 Fenwick)
  • 1904 Somerville City Directory (first listing at 29 and 31 Fenwick)
  • Architectural and material features consistent with 1903 construction
  • Massachusetts Historical Commission documentation of Somerville's Late Industrial Period (1870–1915) housing pattern

The building is a well-preserved example of a Winter Hill speculative duplex constructed at the early end of Somerville's post-1900 residential infill wave. It retains significant original architectural character and evidence of the era's evolving residential building technologies.
